Part one

The opening, in six moves

Every first prompt Nikhil gave had the same shape. He doesn't recite it like a template. It's just what a clear brief sounds like when you say it out loud, and SAGE does its best work when all six moves are in it.

Set the scene

"As you know, we've built…" · "I'm on the call with Nikhil, the creator of ACE…"

SAGE starts out knowing what exists, who is in the room, and what already happened ("the error we logged prior").

Say what you see

"It says voice, but the voice isn't transcribed."

Say what is happening, where it is on screen, and what should be happening instead. A screenshot or a version number beats an adjective.

Give the so-that

"So that we can refine further." · "So I can understand and approve."

The purpose sets the depth. The same audit means something different when it feeds a decision than when it feeds a build.

Analysis before action

"Do an analysis first." · "Would there be any conflicts?"

When something is complex or new, he asks for the read before the change. On small actions SAGE already knows, he goes straight to "run it now."

Name the shape

"An HTML map, so I can see it at a glance."

Tell it what the answer should look like: a visual map, A to J in plain language, a report in your inbox. You get the answer in a form you can use.

Invite the upside

"Add any insights or opportunities." · "What would you build next?"

He always leaves room for SAGE to see more than he asked for. That's where most of the good findings came from.

Part two

The loop, from plan to proof

Once the opening lands, he runs the same steps every time, in this order. The highlighted step is the one that does the most work.

Step 1

Plan

"Do an analysis first and figure out the best plan."

Step 2

Pressure test

"Pressure test all your assumptions one more time."

Step 3

Read it back plain

"Each one, A to J, in simple layman's terms."

Step 4

Approve with fences

"We're happy with your plan. Keep any hooks disconnected."

Step 5

Run it

"Go ahead and run it now."

Step 6

Prove it

"We're running a test for three days."

The step you skipped

Looking at the /va findings, you went straight to "go hit all of these 14 things, highest priority to the bottom." Nikhil stopped you: "I'll tell you exactly what I would do." Then came the pressure test. It made seven changes to the report before a single line of the skill was touched.

Part three

By what you're building

Ten situations from the call. Each card has what Nikhil actually said (lightly cleaned of repeated words), your version tuned to how you talk, and why it works. Words in [brackets] are yours to fill. The copy button takes the whole prompt.

01

Auditing something you built

Audit

A skill has grown and you're no longer sure everything it does, or you're about to add features to it.

Nikhil said

"As you know, we've built out a VA skill that has multiple capabilities. I want to understand all the capabilities so that we can refine further. Go ahead and audit what we've built, the features and the functionality, and design an HTML map and report, so I'm able to see at a glance the actual structure and workflows of the VA skill and any sub-skills. Also add any notes, insights or opportunities to refine it further."

Your version

"As you know, we've built [the CMO]. I want to see everything it does so we can sharpen it. Audit it and give me an HTML map I can read at a glance: the structure, the workflows, and where it's leaking. Add any insights or opportunities. Don't change anything yet."

All six moves are in it, and "don't change anything yet" keeps the audit an audit. His reason for doing it before adding features: if you build on top of "an energetic leak or a subtle distortion," it causes more problems downstream.

02

Something's off

Debug

A feature isn't doing what it should, an error showed up, or something is quietly stuck.

Nikhil said

"I'm trying to test out the agents view. You can see in the screenshot attached, this task attempted to check five of my emails and then email me, but it's coming up with the safety guardrails error, with both connectors. How can we resolve this? Which connector do we use for my Gmail? We want to unblock this so that it works."

And the short form, while the comms sync crawled: "Is it still in queue, or is there an issue?"

Your version

"Here's what I'm seeing: [what, and where on screen]. What should be happening is [this]. [Screenshot attached. We're on version X. We already tried Y.] Figure out what's going on before you fix anything, and tell me whether it's a queue, a setting, or a real bug."

What you see versus what should happen, plus evidence. "It's broken" makes SAGE guess. "It says voice but there's no transcript, and sync is still spinning" lets it look.

03

Bringing in a new skill or tool

Install

Someone sends you a GitHub link, or you've found a tool you want inside SAGE.

Nikhil said

"I want to integrate this skill into my ACE so that I can map out my system. Can you first check and integrate the skill, and also suggest how this would be useful for me to better visualize my data. And also, would there be any conflicts if we install this skill?"

His standing rule: before installing anything, ask how it would be useful for you and whether it will conflict with anything you already use. For Superpowers he went one step further: "Do an analysis first and figure out the best integration plan."

Your version

"I want to bring [this link] into SAGE so I can [purpose]. Before you install anything: how would it actually help me, would it conflict with anything I already run, and does it add hooks? Keep hooks off unless I say otherwise."

It paid off the same afternoon. Graphify's own installer would have written into CLAUDE.md, hooked itself onto every file read and search, and added a git hook. Asking the conflict question meant five conflicts got closed before any of them could happen.

04

Before any batch of changes

The gate

SAGE has handed you findings, fixes or a plan, and you're about to say "do it all."

Nikhil said

"I would always, in this instance, ask ACE to just pressure test all your assumptions one more time."

"Can you tell me each refinement from A to J in simple layman's terms so I can understand and approve, please?"

Why: "I've skimmed through it, but I don't want to read every single detail, because I just have no capacity for it."

Your version, three lines, in order
  1. "Pressure test all your assumptions one more time."
  2. "Walk me through each change, A to whatever letter, in plain language so I can approve."
  3. "Yes to [all / A, C and D]. Then reread the skill for anything that now contradicts itself, and build it."

There's never a perfect solution, he said, only black-and-white calls and gray ones. The pressure test catches the gray ones while they're still cheap to fix. On /va it changed seven things.

Lines one and two are his words. "Reread for contradictions" is SAGE's addition, taken from his own rule in card 09. He didn't say it in this sequence.

05

Granting permission, or lifting a rule

Permission

A guardrail is doing its job, and you want this one thing to get through.

Nikhil said

"Let's rerun that same agent task again, but specifically give it permission to send the email. And it has permission to send emails to my personal inbox only, for now."

And the principle: "You could explicitly tell it to break its rule. If you do that, just be very specific about that."

Your version

"You have permission to [exact action], to [exact recipient or scope] only, for now. Everything else stays as it is."

Exact scope plus "for now" makes it a grant, not a new habit. This one is enforced as well as written down: a guard now blocks any background email that isn't addressed to you.

06

Automating something

Agents

A job you keep doing by hand could run on its own.

Nikhil said

"Go ahead and create a new task in the agent view to run that exact same test. Go set that up for me, and run it now."

"In that initial test, you would run it as a controlled test. We're running a test for three days, and we're going to see how well it functions and performs."

Your version

"Set this up as an agent task: [what it does], [when it runs], and [where the result lands]. Run it once now so we can watch it. Then we run it as a controlled test for three days before I trust it."

An automation is only as good as its first unattended week. Your boundary still applies inside an agent: it can work and draft at any hour, but nothing reaches another person between 5 PM and 6 AM.

07

Setting the horizon

What's next

The fixes are in, and you're ready to think bigger about where something goes.

Nikhil said

"Over the next whatever months or so, this is my goal for how I want the skill to function. What can we build next? Or what suggestions would you say to begin next?"

Your version

"Over the next [30 days], here's how I want [X] to work: [the picture]. What would you build next, and what's the smallest test that proves it works?"

You give the destination, SAGE proposes the route. Your answer on the call is the model: refine /va for two to four weeks, then turn it into an agent that sweeps through the working day, after a three-day controlled test.

08

Curiosity before a fix

Why

Something is missing or misclassified, and you don't yet know why.

Nikhil said

"Why isn't the CMO project that you're working on being picked up as a project inside project view? I'm just kind of curious what comes up."

Your version

"Why isn't [X] showing up as [Y]? I'm curious what you find. Don't fix it yet."

Asking why gets SAGE to show its logic before it patches anything. Here it showed that a skill you're developing is also a project, and Nikhil took that back to fix in ACE for everyone.

09

Keeping a skill clean after edits

Hygiene

You've changed a skill on the fly a few times, or you notice you're telling SAGE the same thing twice.

Nikhil said

"We can on the fly change something in the file. Unless after that change a proper review is done, just to ensure there's no conflicts or clobbering, sometimes one line from earlier in the skill that didn't change can create confusion."

"You could create that as an automated agent that runs once a week: review the entire VA skill stack to identify where there might be some conflicts or areas for optimization, and email you a report."

Your version

"Read [the skill] top to bottom and tell me anywhere it contradicts itself or still carries an old rule. Show me the lines before you change anything."

This is why you sometimes have to tell SAGE twice. Near the top, /va said every Notion write needed your yes first. Further down it said the opposite, because you lifted that gate on September 29 and the old line stayed. A fresh session reads the old rule first.

10

When the machine is struggling

Machine

ACE feels slow, the vitals ring says hot, or the fan won't quit.

Nikhil said

"I notice right now that the vitals meter in ACE is saying that my system is running hot. Can you do an analysis of my computer and also ACE itself and what processes are running? And can you suggest any optimizations that ACE and Nikhil, the creator of ACE, could make to support performance on Windows and my device?"

Your version

"The vitals say I'm running hot. Look at the whole machine, not just ACE: what's eating memory, what's ACE's share of it, and what would you change, on my side and on Nikhil's?"

He asked for both halves. The answer: memory at 92%, mostly not ACE, plus a real cold-start cost inside ACE that went to Nikhil as Finding 14.

Part four

How the /va build happened

The full sequence from the call, in order, so you can run it again on any skill. The two highlighted steps are where the build turned.

  1. You named the need plainly

    How do I look at what the VA does right now, what the current commands are, so I know how to make any tweaks?

    You didn't say "fix it" or "improve it." You asked to see it, and that's what set up everything after.

  2. A fresh chat for one job

    Nikhil had you open a new chat: I'll tell you exactly how I talk. One job per chat keeps the context clean. (He also pointed out that a green tick on a tab means /close has run.)

  3. The brief

    The audit prompt from card 01, ending in an HTML map and report with opportunities to refine.

  4. Why HTML

    Nikhil: If I'm building something that has some level of complexity, I like to build HTML little experiences so I can visualize and see things, not purely in text. You: I have to see it in order to understand it at a deeper level.

  5. A map for SAGE too

    While that ran, he had you install Graphify with the card 03 prompt, so the skill also got a graph SAGE can navigate. As you're building and refining the skill, get it to reference that graph. It's like a visual index.

    97 nodes · 203 edges · 6 clusters · hooks never installed

  6. Reading the findings together

    The map came back with the architecture, the client journey, how each job runs, who's allowed to do what, and where each fact lives. It also had 21 findings and 10 refinement options. The ones you walked through: client names reaching GitHub, the skill contradicting itself on the Notion gate, the queue drifting (long rows, status words outside the defined set, twelve loop types), a template still pointing at the connector that strips the logo, reschedule searching only one mailbox, and nothing triggering the 48-hour check-in.

  7. A second, independent pass

    Superpowers went in with hooks off, and its first job was a code review of /va. It found the same core problems. Those are all covered. When two separate passes agree, you can trust the findings.

  8. The moment

    You: Go hit all of these 14 things from highest priority all the way down to the bottom. Nikhil: I'll tell you exactly what I would do. Then: Pressure test all your assumptions one more time.

  9. What the pressure test changed

    It retracted one claim (a miscount that was SAGE's own), corrected three (a spelling that was already right, a load size of 58 KB rather than 75 KB, and a scheduler route the sweep turned out to have), sharpened the privacy finding against what was actually on GitHub, and added two new findings.

    1 retracted · 3 corrected · 1 sharpened · 2 added · 0 lines of the skill touched yet

  10. A to J, in plain language

    Ten refinements read back simply enough to decide on. Your answer: Based on the work we've done so far and the pressure test results, I am saying yes to all changes.

  11. Shipped

    /va v0.4, committed at 3:09 PM: queue format v2 with a helper script that enforces it, a tracked-file privacy rule, rules split from evidence, rebuilt Sage templates, 12-hour times and the work-window filter on Calendly, and the lightning list return signal.

    all ten refinements applied

  12. What's next

    Refine for two to four weeks, then turn it into an agent that sweeps through the working day, after a three-day controlled test. Nikhil offered to be on deck for that.
